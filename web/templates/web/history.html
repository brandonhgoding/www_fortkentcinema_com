Nearly everything below comes from one published article by a named archivist; a page about real families in a small town should say so before it starts telling their story, not after. {% endcomment %} <aside class="history-sourcenote"> <p> Most of what follows is drawn from <strong>Lisa Ornstein’s “Going to the Movies in Fort Kent,”</strong> published in <em>Echoes: The Northern Maine Journal</em>, No. 48, April/June 2000. Ornstein was director of the Acadian Archives / Archives acadiennes at the University of Maine at Fort Kent. Where something comes from somewhere else — or where the record disagrees with itself — this page says so. </p> </aside> <section class="history-section"> <h2 class="history-section__title">A barn on the Stevens homestead</h2> <p> In 1904 the Fort Kent chapter of the Modern Woodmen of America, a fraternal benefit society, bought the Samuel Stevens homestead and opened up its refurbished barn as a community hall. Woodmen Hall, as everyone called it, did a bit of everything: club gatherings, town meetings, weddings, dances, basketball games, minstrel shows. </p> <p> Around 1917 its owners installed projection equipment and added one more line to the roster — <strong>The Savoy Theatre</strong>. The first film shown was D. W. Griffith’s <em>The Birth of a Nation</em>. That barn stood where the cinema’s building and parking lot are today, which means Fort Kent has been watching movies on the same patch of ground for more than a century. </p> </section> <section class="history-section"> <h2 class="history-section__title">A dime, a folding chair, and a piano</h2> <p> The early Savoy was a make-do operation. For a dime, moviegoers sat on “rickety wooden folding chairs placed on the flat main floor or in the balcony,” as Ornstein has it, and watched silent pictures while a local pianist played beside the screen. She names three of them: <strong>Mrs. George Bradbury</strong>, <strong>Theresa Michaud</strong> and <strong>Burdina Wheelock</strong>. </p> <p> Ownership churned. The Savoy changed hands in 1920 to Aroostook County Theaters Incorporated of Houlton; by 1928 it belonged to Publix Theatres, a national chain; a few years later to Julia M. Powers and Raymond R. Johnston, who ran houses in Caribou and Fort Fairfield, who in turn joined a Saint John, New Brunswick chain with more than fifty theaters either side of the border. A photograph survives of the building standing in the floodwater of 1923. </p> <p> By the early 1930s it had stopped being a hall that showed films and become a cinema: <strong>533 seats</strong>, a properly raked floor, a different picture six nights a week — and wired for sound. The Savoy’s first talkie was <em>The Jazz Singer</em>. </p> </section> {% comment %} The playbill carousel. They are set again here from the originals — the printer’s punctuation, his inconsistent quote marks, and the weeks he abandoned them altogether. </p> <p class="history-playbills__lead"> A different picture every night, six nights a week, changed every Monday. Warner Oland’s <em>The Black Camel</em> reached Fort Kent on June 23, two days after it opened nationally; Bela Lugosi’s <em>Dracula</em> played on a Saturday in June. This was not a town getting films late. </p> <p class="history-playbills__lead"> Two things are visible only because all six survive together. Every title in April carries the line <strong>ALL TALKING</strong> — and not one does from May onward. Somewhere between April 18 and May 18, 1931, sound stopped being worth advertising in Fort Kent. And on July 8 the Savoy ran <em>L’Énigmatique Monsieur Parkes</em>, a French-language production — eight years before the parish opened its own theater down the road. </p> <div class="history-playbills__stage"> {% for pb in playbills %} <div class="history-playbills__item" {% if not forloop.first %}x-cloak{% endif %} x-show="i === {{ forloop.counter0 }}"> {% include "partials/playbill.html" with pb=pb pbindex=forloop.counter0 %} </div> {% endfor %} </div> {% if playbills|length > 1 %} <nav class="history-playbills__nav" x-cloak aria-label="Playbills"> <button type="button" class="history-playbills__arrow" @click="i = (i - 1 + n) % n" aria-label="Previous playbill">←</button> <span class="history-playbills__count" aria-live="polite"> <span x-text="i + 1"></span> of <span x-text="n"></span> </span> <button type="button" class="history-playbills__arrow" @click="i = (i + 1) % n" aria-label="Next playbill">→</button> </nav> {% endif %} {% comment %} The sheets are undated. Ornstein's caption estimates "circa 1936" for the April 13 one; six sheets together make 1931 close to certain, and the page says why rather than just asserting a different year. {% endcomment %} <p class="history-playbills__dating"> None of the sheets carries a year. The published caption on one of them estimates 1936. But all six run Monday to Saturday on dates that fit 1931, every one of the thirty-six films was released in 1930 or 1931, and several arrived within days of opening nationally — so these are first-run weeks in 1931, not a revival. </p> </section> <section class="history-section"> <h2 class="history-section__title">The theater under the convent</h2> <p> Fort Kent’s second cinema opened around 1939, and it was a parish project. Led by St. Louis Parish priest <strong>Rev. Aimé Giguère</strong>, volunteers converted the parish hall in the basement of the convent into a full <strong>325-seat</strong> theater — ticket booth, raked seating, concession stand and all — to raise money for building the People’s Benevolent Hospital, known today as Northern Maine Medical Center. </p> <p> The <em>Petites sœurs franciscaines</em> lived upstairs, with their chapel sitting directly above the theater. Sister Gloria Ouellette found evening prayer the hardest hour of the day: the soundtrack came up through the floor distinctly enough to tell one actor’s voice from another. For a decade or so, Ingrid Bergman, Jimmy Stewart and Lash La Rue were part of convent life. </p> <p> Doors opened every night of the week, with a matinee on Saturdays. Admission was a dime, and a penny more once the war was on. The bill was mostly Hollywood, with French-language pictures worked in. Delsie Charette remembered French-Canadian classics bringing whole families in from the country. It closed in the early 1950s. </p> <p> The two houses were rivals, and not politely: by the early 1940s the Savoy’s owners were working to squeeze the Community out. It ended in 1945, when Ashland native <strong>Charles A. H. Brooks</strong> bought the Savoy and went into partnership with the hospital corporation instead — handing it half the Savoy’s receipts and taking over management of both theaters. </p> </section> <section class="history-section"> <h2 class="history-section__title">Three hundred and fifty cars</h2> <p> In the summer of 1951 Brooks opened the 240-speaker Fort Kent Drive-In on the Caribou Road, again splitting the takings with the hospital fund. Between the Savoy and the drive-in, ticket receipts raised roughly <strong>$206,000</strong> toward the People’s Benevolent Hospital, which opened in 1952. A hospital paid for, a dime at a time, by people going to the pictures. </p> <p> The drive-in outlasted the Community Theatre by decades, run by Brooks, Dick Harvey, Clarence Levesque and Paul Dubois until 1989. Levesque remembered the All-Nighters best — dusk-to-dawn screenings on summer holiday weekends, 350 to 400 cars in the field. </p> </section> <section class="history-section"> <h2 class="history-section__title">The Century, and after</h2> <p> The Savoy found its last owners in 1969 — a local group incorporated as Century Theater, Inc., with <strong>W. J. Ouellette</strong> at its head. They put up a modern building next door, and the old one came down on <strong>December 9, 1969</strong>. The new house opened a week later as <strong>the Century</strong>, named for the town’s centennial. </p> <p> Jeffrey Plourde bought it in 1988 and ran it as Plourde’s Century Theater. It later passed to the Paradis family, who kept it going until 2024, when it changed hands again and took its current name: <strong>Fort Kent Cinema</strong>. </p> <p class="history-closer"> The two screens are called <strong>the Century</strong> and <strong>the Savoy</strong>. </p> </section> {% comment %} Single-source material, fenced off and labelled. The Gem appears in no newspaper, no database, and not in Ornstein — only in the Society's own exhibit, from one poster. That is worth telling and worth flagging. {% endcomment %} <section class="history-section history-open"> <h2 class="history-section__title">One name we are not sure about</h2> <p> The Fort Kent Historical Society’s centennial exhibit records that before it was the Savoy, the house was called <strong>the Gem</strong>, from about 1917 until 1920 — and that we only know this because a 1918 poster turned up inside a wall. </p> <p> We have not been able to corroborate that anywhere else. Ornstein, writing in 2000, calls the Savoy Fort Kent’s first theater and never mentions the Gem — though she does date the building’s first change of ownership to exactly 1920, which fits. If that poster surfaced after she wrote, both accounts can be true. We would rather show you the loose thread than tie it off neatly. </p> <p class="history-open__ask"> If you know something about the Gem — or about any of this — we would genuinely like to hear from you. </p> </section> {% comment %} Trailer-facade pattern, same as partials/now_showing.html and the Silents Synced page: the iframe src stays empty until `playing` flips, so the page makes no request to YouTube unless someone asks.
